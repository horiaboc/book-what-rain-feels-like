# Chapter Twenty: August

---

The first Thursday without a job, I woke at seven because my body still expected to, and then lay in bed for twenty minutes doing nothing, which I recommend.

Freedom has a specific texture in the mornings. The apartment felt different in daylight — or I was in it differently, present in a way I had not been since first arriving in Berlin, when everything was still new enough to look at. I noticed the way the light moved through the kitchen window in the hour after sunrise, which it had presumably been doing for years. I noticed that Diogenes, having been moved to the sill some months ago, appeared to be flourishing in a way that suggested he had known what he needed before I did.

I made coffee. I sat with it. Neither of these things was remarkable and both were, slightly.

---

Mia sent a message on the Wednesday: *I heard. I hope you're not being an idiot about it.* Then, a minute later: *I also think you were right. Don't tell Steffen I said that.*

I sent back something brief and reassuring. She replied with a single full stop, which from Mia constitutes complete satisfaction.

On the Friday I called my parents in Heidelberg. My mother asked practical questions in the methodical way she always does: what are my options, what is the timeline, have I spoken to anyone. She asked about Iris too — carefully, as she does — whether this would make things harder between us, whether Iris was all right about it. I told her Iris had been steadying, not worried, and I watched my mother not quite believe me and then decide to. My father said he was glad I hadn't stayed somewhere that didn't deserve me, which is the kind of thing he says and which I have learned to receive as the specific form of love that it is. I told them both I was fine, which was true, and that I was not in a hurry about what came next, which they accepted with varying degrees of equanimity.

After I hung up I sat for a moment with the particular quality of that call — the ease of it, the fact that nothing needed to be explained. I have been lucky in this way and I know it.

---

Iris told me about the Amsterdam assignment that Saturday over breakfast.

She had been covering Dutch civic AI governance for several months — algorithm transparency, the ethics of automated municipal decision-making — and this was the natural conclusion of it: a week in Amsterdam, two interviews she had been working to arrange for some time. Someone from the city's digital office who had been involved in Amsterdam's public algorithm register, and a researcher at a technology and society institute called Waag. It was a good piece, she said, and she needed to be there to do it properly.

She said this with the matter-of-fact clarity she brings to anything she is certain about. Then she looked at me, slightly carefully, and said: "I was thinking you might want to come."

I said yes before she finished the sentence.

---

We booked a flat on the Bloemgracht — a canal in the Jordaan, quiet, the kind of address that looks slightly too picturesque in the photographs and turns out to be exactly as it looks. Iris said the Jordaan was the neighbourhood she had always preferred in Amsterdam, even as a student when she couldn't afford it. She had lived near the Leidseplein then — one of the streets behind the Paradiso — and had spent a lot of time walking north into the Jordaan on weekends, the way you adopt a neighbourhood by walking through it until it feels like yours.

I listened to her describe it — the cross-canals in the afternoon light, the Saturday market at the Noordermarkt, a bookshop on the Spui she had spent a significant portion of her student budget in — with the attention I always pay when she talks about a place she knows. She is good at this, as she is good at most forms of description: precise, unhurried, the detail landing in the right order. There was a cheese shop on Haarlemmerdijk she had bought from every week. There was a particular view of the Westerkerk tower from a bridge she always used on the route between her flat and the university.

I was, I found, looking forward to seeing it all.

The trip was ten days away. I spent the remaining time in a way I hadn't in years: without urgency, without the specific weight of a problem I was carrying and not yet ready to set down. Whatever came next could wait. The Bloemgracht was waiting, and Iris was going to show me Amsterdam, and it was August, and for now that was enough.
