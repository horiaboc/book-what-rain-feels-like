# Chapter Thirty-Two: What My Hands Did

---

We left the flat at eight forty-five with coffee to go — paper cups, the kind with a cardboard sleeve that is never quite enough. Jonas had found a place two streets over that opened early. The morning was grey and mild, the Bloemgracht quiet at that hour, a cyclist going past with the unhurried confidence of someone who has done this particular route for years.

The UMC is in the southeast of the city. We had allowed an hour and a half, which was enough. Jonas had the route on his phone. I knew the general direction — or I believed I did, which is a distinction I had begun to pay attention to.

We walked east, then south. The city was waking around us in the particular way of Amsterdam mornings: gradual, unhurried, proceeding according to its own logic. I held my coffee and thought about the appointment — what they would ask, what I would say, whether there was a version of the morning that ended with something I could understand and hold.

I was not thinking about the van.

---

The alley was on the Kloveniersburgwal, a narrow passage between two buildings that cut through to a parallel street. Jonas had taken it because the map showed it as a shortcut. It was the kind of passage that exists in every old city — functional, unremarkable, slightly too quiet.

We were halfway through when I heard the engine.

The van came in from the far end — dark, a side door already sliding open before it had fully stopped. Two men out before I had processed that they were moving toward us. Large, efficient, the specific economy of people who have done this before and intend to do it quickly.

I am trying, now, to reconstruct what happened. I find I cannot do it fully. There is the moment I heard the engine, and then there is a moment some seconds later when one of the men was on the ground and the other had stepped back and Jonas had his hand on my arm and was saying my name.

In between: my body did something.

My coffee was on the ground. I don't remember putting it there. My hands were in a position that I recognised, distantly, as a guard stance — weight forward, centre low. The man on the ground was getting up slowly, recalibrating. The other was watching me with an expression I did not expect: not aggression, but something more like reassessment.

From the van's passenger seat, through the open door, a woman was watching. Dark hair, early thirties, eyes that were doing rapid calculations. She did not get out.

"Iris." Jonas's hand on my arm, tighter. "We go. Now."

We went.

---

I am not certain how many streets we covered before Jonas pulled me into the doorway of a printer's shop, its shutters still half-down, a narrow gap of shadow between the door and the building's edge. We pressed into it. My heart was doing something I could observe but not quite regulate.

We stayed there for eleven minutes. I counted without meaning to.

No one came.

Eventually Jonas exhaled — slowly, deliberately — and looked at me.

"Are you hurt?"

"No," I said. And then, because it was true and seemed important to say: "I don't know what I did."

He looked at me carefully. "You put one of them on the ground."

"I know. I don't — I know the movements. Wing chun, judo. I've trained since I was ten." I looked at my hands. They looked entirely ordinary. "I've never done it like that. That quickly. I didn't think."

"Your body thought."

"Yes," I said. "That's what frightens me."

He was quiet for a moment. Then: "It also got us out of an alley with two large men and a van. So."

I looked at him. He said it with the careful dryness he uses when he is frightened and choosing not to be frightened yet, and I found I was grateful for it.

"The woman in the van," I said. "Front seat. She didn't get out."

"I saw her."

"She was directing. Or observing."

"Both, probably." He checked his phone. "We've missed the start of the appointment. We could still go — explain, reschedule from there."

I thought about this. The UMC was still twenty minutes away, and whoever had been in that van knew our route, had known we were heading somewhere in that direction this morning. The route we had planned was not private anymore.

"Not today," I said.

He nodded. He had expected this answer. "We need to think about where we go from here."

---

We found a café on the Staalstraat that was open and full enough to be anonymous — the particular safety of a room full of people who are not interested in you. We took a table at the back. I held my new coffee — Jonas had ordered at the counter, insisted, returned with two cups and a look that said he was managing himself carefully — and tried to sit with what had happened.

"The krav maga," Jonas said, eventually. "The wing chun."

"I've trained since I was ten," I said again. "The krav maga was in Amsterdam, a short course. The rest was childhood. It was a long time ago."

"But it came back."

"It came back as if it had never left." I looked at the table. "More than that. Faster than it should have. More — efficient than the training would explain."

He was quiet. I could see him running something in his mind, choosing how to frame it.

"You know," he said, "there is one explanation that accounts for the bone density, the healing, and now this."

I looked at him.

"You're a spy," he said. "Government programme. Enhanced. The memories are a cover. The journalism is a cover. You've been activated."

I looked at him for a long moment. "That is an absurd theory."

"It's no more absurd than genetic anomaly."

"It's considerably more absurd."

"The bone density," he said. "The cortex connectivity. The healing. The krav maga that isn't quite krav maga." He tilted his head. "I'm just saying. The data fits."

"I am not a spy," I said.

"You didn't know you could do that twenty minutes ago either."

I had no good answer to this. I picked up my coffee.

"If you are," he said, "I want to be clear that I am still fully on your side. I assume your mission is something reasonable. Climate. Public health."

"I am not a spy."

"Noted," he said. And then, more quietly: "We'll figure it out."

I believed him. That was the strange thing — I believed him completely, even sitting in a café in a city where someone had just tried to put me in a van, even with my hands still feeling like something that didn't entirely belong to me. I believed him, and the believing settled something.

---

We stayed for an hour. Jonas worked through the options methodically, which is what he does when the situation is bad and getting methodical is the only available form of control. The flat: we needed our things. The question was whether they were watching it.

"I go back alone," he said. "There's an entrance on the side street — a service door, I noticed it the first day. I can get in that way, get the bags, be out in ten minutes."

"That's not—"

"You stay somewhere public," he said. "The Waterlooplein market. Busy, open, surrounded by people. I'll be back in forty minutes."

I started to say something and he looked at me with a quality that was not unkind but was also not open to revision.

"You put someone on the ground this morning," he said. "I am aware that you can take care of yourself. I am still going back to the flat alone, because one person draws less attention than two, and because one person moving quietly draws less notice than two." A pause. "Also someone needs to hold the table."

I looked around. We were in a café.

"Different table," he said. "There's a place by the Waterlooplein that does good appelgebak. I'll find you there."

---

He found me there, forty-three minutes later, with both bags and a slightly out-of-breath quality he was not acknowledging.

"Suspect vehicle," he said, sitting down. "Dark estate, two streets from the flat. Couldn't see who was inside."

"So we don't go back."

"Not today." He flagged the server. "We need a plan and somewhere quiet to make one. I think the place to start is somewhere you know — somewhere with people around, where you have a reason to be."

I thought about it. The options were limited. The journalism school had already shown its gaps. The colleagues I had tried to recall were faces without certainty behind them.

Then: "De Correspondent," I said. "The Amsterdam office. I worked there before Berlin, briefly. I know people there — or I remember knowing people there." I paused. "Public space. Open plan. Editorial meetings in the afternoon, always people around."

Jonas nodded slowly. "It's still early afternoon. We go today — during office hours, in public." He looked at me. "And we see what holds."

Outside, the Waterlooplein was doing its busy, cheerful thing. A market stall was selling second-hand books. A child was arguing with absolute conviction about something that mattered enormously to her and no one else.

I held my coffee and watched the city and tried to decide how frightened I was. The honest answer was: more than I had shown, and less than I might have expected. Both things were true at once, which seemed, under the circumstances, like a reasonable place to be.
