# Chapter Eleven: The Party

---

The party was in Prenzlauer Berg, which already told you something about who was throwing it.

Lukas, a photographer at her paper who had been there since before she arrived — he had a large studio apartment near Kollwitzplatz that could hold forty people if you removed the furniture and everyone agreed to stand close together. People did both. There was a playlist that went from acceptable to strange around midnight, wine in the kind of bottles that don't have labels, and the particular density of conversation that happens when a room full of people in their late twenties has collectively decided the evening matters.

Iris had asked me three days before. "A friend's thing. You don't have to." The way she said *you don't have to* was the way people say things they'd prefer you to say yes to. I said yes.

---

I arrived twenty minutes late, which was still early.

She found me at the door before I'd properly entered — she had been watching for me, I understood, though she didn't say so. Dark dress, the good coat over her arm. Her hair down, which I hadn't seen before and which I spent a disproportionate amount of the next two hours not thinking about.

"You came," she said.

"You said I didn't have to."

"That's not the same as not wanting you to."

"No," I agreed. "It isn't."

---

I am not good at parties in the general sense. I am capable of them — I can locate a corner and a conversation and remain for the required duration without visible distress — but I have never felt that a room full of noise and strangers is the natural habitat of anything I want to be. What I usually do is find the one interesting person and talk to them until it becomes acceptable to leave.

That evening I had a considerable advantage.

We moved through the apartment in the loose way of two people who aren't quite together and aren't quite not, navigating the gap between the two with varying degrees of grace. I met Lukas, who shook my hand with the confidence of someone who had heard about me, which was interesting because I had not heard that he had heard about me. I met two colleagues of Iris's from the paper, and a structural engineer named Bram who was visiting from Rotterdam and had strong opinions about the Nijmegen flood diversions. I had moderate opinions about the Nijmegen flood diversions, which was enough for a good conversation and a better exit.

At some point I lost her to a cluster of journalists in the kitchen.

I stood near the window and watched the room without examining it too closely, which is how I prefer to watch rooms. The street below: Prenzlauer Berg at eleven, still warm with Friday, still going. A couple walking a dog that had no interest in walking. The particular orange of Berlin streetlamps on a night that hadn't decided about rain.

Then she was beside me.

"You were doing the window thing," she said.

"The window thing."

"When you go quiet and look at something far away. You do it when you need to reset."

She had noticed this. I had not noticed she had noticed this.

"Bram had opinions," I said.

"Bram always has opinions." She stood beside me at the window and looked at the street. Close — the apartment was full and the window alcove was not large, and there was the simple fact of her presence beside me in a way that registered differently in a room full of people than it did on a canal at midnight. "Are you glad you came?"

"Ask me at the end," I said. "I'll have better data."

She smiled at that, which made my data immediately better.

---

An hour later the apartment had reached the particular late-party density where conversations compress and migrate and people begin to locate their coats without fully committing to the idea. Iris was saying something to Lukas across the room and I was threading through toward the kitchen when the crowd shifted — a sudden press of people moving between rooms — and my hand found the small of her back.

One second. Maybe two.

She didn't move away. Neither did I.

The crowd redistributed. I found the kitchen. I got a glass of water and drank it looking at someone's collection of mismatched mugs on an open shelf and thought: there it is. The thing I had been moving toward without inspecting the route. There it is.

---

We left around midnight.

Outside: cold, the rain that hadn't decided finally deciding. Wet cobblestones under the Kollwitzplatz lindens, the city doing its usual thing of being beautiful in the dark without making anything of it. She had her coat on now; I had mine. We stood on the steps for a moment while the party continued behind the lit windows.

"Good data?" she said.

"Very good," I said.

She was close. The air was cold and the light from the windows was warm and she was looking at me in the way she had at the canal — the way I had been filing and returning to for weeks — and this time I did not decide to wait for certainty.

I kissed her in the rain outside Lukas's apartment on the last Friday of March.

She kissed me back, which was the only data point that mattered.

---

We walked to Schönhauser Allee and took the U2 south together and rode it in the particular silence of two people who have just changed something and are deciding how to hold it. She sat beside me and our shoulders touched in the way of a train car, which is ordinary, except that nothing about the next hour was ordinary.

At Gleisdreieck she looked at me.

"Walk again sometime?" she said.

I understood she was quoting us.

"Walk again sometime," I said.

She got off at Nollendorfplatz. I stayed on until Hermannstraße, changed, and took the U8 the short way home — four stops, six minutes, the route I have taken a thousand times. I arrived at the apartment in a state I don't have a previous record of.

Diogenes regarded me from the sill.

"I know," I told him.

He turned back to the window. Some data speaks for itself.
