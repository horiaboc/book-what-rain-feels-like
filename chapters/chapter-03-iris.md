# Chapter Three: New Coordinates

---

The apartment still smells of paint.

I have been here six days and I have unpacked most of the boxes, though not all — there is a rhythm to settling into a new place, and I have learned to trust it. The last boxes are always the ones that contain the things you were not sure you needed but could not bring yourself to leave behind: a ceramic bowl from a market in Haarlem, three books I have read twice and will probably read again, a lamp with a slightly bent shade that I keep meaning to fix and never do.

The lamp is now on the floor beside the window, bent shade and all. It gives the room a quality of afternoon even in the morning. I find I prefer this.

Berlin is louder than Amsterdam. Not unpleasantly — differently. Amsterdam has the water running through everything, holding the city at a certain pitch. Here the sound is drier, more abrupt: trams, construction somewhere in the middle distance, the particular clatter of a city that was broken and put back together and is still, perhaps, working out the shape of itself. I have already filled two pages of notes. I cannot help it.

---

I think of my desk at *De Correspondent* sometimes. The view of the Westerdok from the third floor, the coffee machine that ground its own beans and smelled like the best argument for Monday mornings. Vera, who sat beside me and had opinions about everything and was usually right. I left all of it for this — a city where I know no one, a position I am not yet sure I deserve, a room that still smells of someone else's renovation.

I do not regret it. That is not quite the same as being certain.

The new role is at a small bureau of an international outlet called *Meridian*. My brief is civic and political with a particular focus on technology and ethics — the kind of stories that matter to the people who read them and that most people never read. I have always wanted to write those stories. I am aware this makes me either principled or naïve, and I have not yet determined which. Probably both, in proportions that will only become clear later.

I also miss cycling. Berlin has the infrastructure for it, but I haven't got a bicycle yet, and walking distances that should take fifteen minutes somehow take forty. This is not a complaint. It is an observation.

---

In the afternoon I assemble the bookshelf.

It is flat-pack — the kind that comes with an instruction sheet covered in diagrams and a small bag of parts that always seems to contain one more screw than necessary. I look at the instructions briefly, set them aside. The logic of it is apparent from the components themselves; the sheet only adds noise. Forty minutes and it is done. I transfer the books from the floor in the arrangement I had already worked out: journalism and ethics on the left, fiction and poetry on the right, the undecided in the middle where they can wait until they know what they are.

The extra screw I leave on the windowsill. It seems the right place for things that don't yet have a home.

---

In the evening I walk.

Kreuzberg first, then further east, following the canal south and crossing somewhere I stop keeping track of, into streets that feel less arranged, more inhabited. A neighborhood of Turkish bakeries and record shops and a Vietnamese restaurant with steamed-up windows and a queue that starts before seven. People in the specific way of a Tuesday in November — not festive, just present, the ordinary business of being in a city.

I take notes I will probably not use. Observations about a mural I don't yet have context for, a man arguing on the phone in three languages without appearing to notice the transitions, the way Berlin pedestrians treat red lights as suggestions. It is not a story yet. It is the material that might become one, which is a different and more interesting thing.

Walking back, I pass a gate left open onto a courtyard — a bicycle leaning against the far wall, a bare tree, a lit window on the fourth floor of the building opposite. Someone's ordinary evening, visible for a moment through someone else's open gate.

I stop for a moment longer than makes sense.

Then I walk on. I don't know why I stopped.

---

In bed, I reach for a memory that doesn't quite arrive.

Not Amsterdam — somewhere earlier, or perhaps later. A garden, possibly. A voice I can't place. It comes to the edge and then disperses, the way some things do when you reach for them directly rather than waiting. I have always been like this, I think — certain memories don't hold their shape under examination. You can't look at them straight.

I leave it alone. It usually comes back on its own.

The apartment smells of paint and the lamp gives the ceiling a soft amber quality and somewhere in the building someone is cooking something that takes garlic seriously.

I am not unhappy.
