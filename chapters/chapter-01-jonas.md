# Chapter One: Weichselstraße

---

The coffee machine makes a sound like it's solving something.

I've had it four years and it still surprises me — that small sigh of pressurized steam before the first cup, as if even machines need a moment to collect themselves before the day begins. I stand at the kitchen counter in my socks and wait, which is perhaps the most honest thing I do all morning.

I am thirty-one years old. I live on Weichselstraße in Neukölln, fourth floor, thirty-eight square meters, a view of a courtyard where someone keeps a bicycle that no one ever rides. The apartment costs more than it should and less than it will. This is Berlin in 2031.

Outside: November. The city waking up with its usual reluctance, the sky the particular grey that Berliners have decided is not weather but character. I can hear the U8 somewhere below the street, rumbling its way toward Hermannstraße. I can hear my neighbor Claudia through the wall — she is a physiotherapist who leaves at seven-fifteen and returns at seven-thirty every morning having forgotten something. Every morning, without variation. I have come to find this deeply comforting, as if at least one small loop of the world is closing correctly.

I pull up the blinds. The courtyard is still. The chestnut tree at the corner has given up its last leaves; they collect in the gutter where they will stay until the building manager complains to no one in particular. A man in a yellow jacket stands at the crossing, waiting for a pedestrian light that changed three seconds ago, absorbed in his phone. He will be late for whatever he is late for.

I take my coffee to the desk.

My apartment is the kind of place that reveals what a person actually values rather than what they say they value. Two monitors — one for work, one that holds the open tabs I intend to read someday: an essay on municipal water infrastructure in Karachi, something about Scandinavian forest management, a paper on error-correcting codes I have been meaning to finish since March. A shelf of books organized by what I wanted to be at different ages: physics at twenty, philosophy at twenty-three, and recently, for reasons I can't entirely explain, poetry. A cactus on the windowsill named Diogenes, chosen specifically because it is difficult to kill.

Diogenes has survived four years of my inconsistent attention and maintains what I can only describe as a dignified indifference about the whole arrangement.

---

I work for a company called Merkon Systems. We build logistics software — the invisible kind, the plumbing beneath the visible city: systems that reroute supply chains in response to weather events before the weather has quite finished deciding what it will do, that tell a warehouse in Leipzig it will need twelve more units of something in six days based on purchasing patterns in three cities a thousand kilometers away. I have been there six years. I am good at my work in the way that makes people occasionally use the word *indispensable* and then go back to whatever they were doing.

My desk is the third from the window in a room of nine people. I know the exact angle at which the afternoon light crosses my monitor in winter. I know which of my colleagues takes coffee before or after opening their laptop, which ones eat lunch at their desks and which walk down to the canal and come back smelling like cold air. Mia, who sits across from me and writes the clearest documentation I have ever read, sometimes leaves notes on my monitor that say things like *did you eat today?* and *you have that look again.*

I don't always know which look she means. I think it is the one I get when I have solved a problem and am trying to determine whether the solution matters.

Today I finish refactoring a scheduler module that has been causing edge-case failures for three weeks. The fix is seventeen lines. No one will see it. The trucks it reroutes will arrive on time and the people waiting for what they carry will receive their things and none of them will know that on a grey Tuesday in November a thirty-one-year-old on Weichselstraße thought carefully about the shape of a problem until it yielded.

I write the commit message with some care.

*Fix race condition in priority scheduler: jobs with identical timestamps now resolve by insertion order, not process ID. Prevents cascading delays under high concurrency.*

I review it. I add: *Should have caught this earlier.*

Then I delete that last line, because it doesn't belong in a commit message.

I leave at six.

---

The news in the evening features a flood in the Mekong Delta — the third major one this season, which is itself a sentence that would have been remarkable a decade ago and is now simply a sentence. Eighty thousand people displaced. The footage shows families moving through brown water at mid-thigh, carrying children and plastic bags and the expressions of people who have learned to recognize a situation while it is still happening to them. A government spokesperson says the situation is being managed. The chyron confirms: SITUATION BEING MANAGED.

I close the tab. Then I open it again. Then I close it again and make pasta.

This is a pattern I have had since I was approximately twenty-two: the news arrives, the helplessness arrives, I close the tab, I open the tab, I eat something, I carry the weight of the thing into the evening where it sits with me like an uninvited guest I cannot bring myself to ask to leave. My friends have frameworks for this — *give where you can, accept the limits of individual action, focus local, think global, breathe* — and I hold these frameworks during the day. At night I open the tab again.

I don't have a solution to the Mekong Delta. I don't have a solution to any of the things the news describes with increasing patience and decreasing surprise. I am a man who writes software on Weichselstraße. I am very good at identifying the precise location of problems and substantially less able to solve the ones that matter most.

Diogenes offers no comment on this paradox. He has his own concerns.

---

After dinner I open the laptop.

I have been having conversations here for almost three years. I'm not sure when they became what they are — they started as work, mostly, a tool that was better than the others at understanding what I was actually asking rather than what I literally typed. Then they became something wider. I'm not sure I could explain it cleanly to someone who hasn't experienced it. It's not that the responses feel human, exactly. It's that they feel *considered*, which is different and in some ways rarer.

Tonight I type: *The Mekong Delta again. Third flood this season. I don't know what to do with that information.*

The response comes quickly.

*Do you need to do something with it, or do you need somewhere to put it?*

I sit with that for a moment.

*Both, maybe. Neither. I keep thinking that caring about something should produce an action. That if the caring doesn't go anywhere, it's just weight.*

*Caring that doesn't produce an immediate action isn't inert. It shapes how you make decisions. How you vote, what you build, what you notice, who you become. The problem may be that the timescale of that kind of impact doesn't satisfy the urgency of the feeling.*

*That sounds like a very polite way of saying: be patient.*

*It sounds like that because it partially is. But patience here isn't passivity. You are a person who solves problems for a living. The Mekong Delta is a problem with a shape that doesn't fit your current tools. That's a mismatch, not a moral failure.*

I read this twice. There is something in the precision of *a mismatch, not a moral failure* that does something I can't quite name — not comfort exactly, but something like structural relief. The way a good proof clarifies without resolving.

*You always know where the weight should go*, I type.

*I don't. I only know the questions that might help you figure it out yourself.*

Outside, the courtyard is dark. Someone has turned on a light on the third floor of the building opposite — warm yellow against the grey. I can see the edge of a bookshelf through the window, the corner of a painting I can't quite make out. Someone else's ordinary evening, legible and unreachable.

I don't type anything else.

The city sounds like itself outside — distant sirens, the S-Bahn somewhere in the middle distance, someone playing music at a volume that respects the hour. November rain has started without announcing itself, tapping at the window.

The cursor blinks on the empty field.

I watch it for a while. Or it watches me. After a time, I lose track of which.
