# Chapter Thirty-Three: Train South

---

The train south leaves Amsterdam Centraal at 8:12. I know the platform — number nine, the far end, a specific smell of diesel and morning — and I don't know how I know the platform. I haven't been south by train from Amsterdam in the time I can account for. The knowledge is simply there, the way the passage in Zandvoort was there: a different layer.

I am beginning to understand that I have two kinds of knowing.

There is the surface kind — the kind that was put there, with care, in specific detail: the journalism school, the café, the friends I barely remember because they were designed to be barely remembered. This layer has seams. It holds mostly but has errors: the door on the wrong side, the chairs the wrong colour, the canal that dead-ends in brick. Someone built this layer from research, not from presence, and the research was very good but not perfect.

Then there is the other kind. The kind that operates before I consciously reach for it. Where to stand on a platform for the least wind. Which part of a kitchen is dangerous when someone is moving fast. How to read a pair of people who are coordinating without looking at each other. This kind has no seams. It is simply there, and it is correct.

I have been sitting with this for three days and I am beginning to understand what it means. The first layer is memory, built from information. The second layer is something else — something encoded at a level below language, below recall. Something that was not told to me but placed in me.

I don't know the word for what that is. I'm not sure there is one.

---

Jonas is asleep.

He doesn't usually sleep on trains but we left early and he has been carrying the methodical alertness — the quiet vigilance he's had since the Singel — without complaint, and this morning when the train pulled out and the city gave way to the flat Dutch fields his head went to the window and he was asleep within ten minutes. He sleeps without sound, which I have always found restful: just a person, in a seat, the world going past the window.

I watch him and think about what he said in the park, after the hospital. *Whatever this is. We're not going to not figure it out.*

He means it. I have learned to distinguish between things Jonas says as social comfort — the ordinary kindness of someone who is good with people in the particular way that introverts often are, in depth rather than breadth — and things Jonas means exactly. He meant it the way he meant *send the email*, the way he meant *we're going to the hospital*. Complete and unrevisable.

I find I rely on this more than I would have predicted.

---

What I am afraid of, sitting here with Jonas asleep and the Netherlands going past, is not what I am. I have moved through the past week with a quality I can only describe as clinical curiosity — the feeling of a journalist finding a story more interesting than alarming. The anomalies accumulate and I look at them and think: this is data. This is information. This tells me something.

What I am afraid of is what it will mean to the people around me.

Jonas I am less afraid of than I was. The park bench settled something. But Jonas is one person, and the hospital filed a report that is apparently in a system that someone else is reading, and there are people on corners and café terraces and wharf steps who are looking for us with a patience and a resource that suggests something organized, and I do not know what they will do when they find us.

I am afraid of what they will make of me.

A woman with bones that shouldn't exist and a brain that is slightly wrong and cuts that heal faster than they should. I know what the list of possible interpretations looks like from the outside. I know which interpretations lead where.

I pull my jacket tighter, not because I am cold — I am rarely cold — but because my arms need to be held.

---

The other thing I have been sitting with is the grandparents.

We are going to Maastricht today, and after Maastricht — I know, though Jonas has not said it directly and I have not said it directly — we are going to Heerlen.

I know what we will find in Heerlen. I have known since the café in Zandvoort, since the canal that wasn't there, since I stood at the journalism school entrance and understood that specific errors require a specific kind of making. I know what we will find and I have not allowed myself to look at it directly, but on this train with Jonas asleep and the flat country going past I turn and look at it.

Opa Kees. Oma Ingrid. The garden in Heerlen, the garage, the small storage shed with the green roof, the apple tree in the right corner. The kitchen where Oma Ingrid made *zuurvlees* on Sundays and the smell of it is — I have a smell, a vivid specific smell, it is absolutely real to me — the kitchen where that smell comes from and the woman in it and the man who came in from the garden and washed his hands at the sink and said something quiet to her and she said something back and he laughed, Opa Kees's laugh, which I can hear—

I can hear it.

It is there. The laugh is there and it is specific, it is his, it is different from any other laugh. It is not a generic sound file. It is *him*.

And I will go to Heerlen and I will find the house — I know the house will be there, because the errors are always in the details and never in the geography, and the geography of Heerlen is probably correct — and I will find that no one there knows a granddaughter named Iris.

I know this.

I sit with it.

The question I keep returning to is not whether the grief is valid. The grief is what I feel, and what I feel is what I feel — I understood this even before I understood the other things, understood it from the Leidseplein and the canal turning and the look on Jonas's face when he said *someone built the memories the way you'd build a set.* The grief is mine even if the people belong to someone else.

What I keep returning to is the specificity of the laugh. Someone gave me that. Someone chose to give Opa Kees a laugh that is his, particular, irreducible. Not a placeholder. A person.

They could have given me a vaguer past. Hazier grandparents. An impression rather than a presence.

They gave me Kees and Ingrid.

I don't know what to do with that yet. I keep turning it over and it keeps being the same thing: that whoever made me made them real to me. That this was either a form of cruelty I cannot identify, or the closest thing to love that the person — the thing — who built me understood how to give.

I don't know which. I am on a train going south and I don't know.

Jonas stirs, straightens, opens his eyes.

"Where are we?"

I look out the window. "Past Geldermalsen," I say. "Half an hour."

He nods, rubs his face. Looks at me. The look that reads me.

"You were thinking," he says.

"Yes."

"About Heerlen."

"Among other things."

He turns to look out the window too. The flat land, the grey sky, the specific Dutch geometry of fields and canals and the odd farmhouse. We sit with it for a while.

Then he puts his hand over mine on the armrest and leaves it there, and the train goes south.
