# Chapter Twenty-Two: Bloemgracht

---

Amsterdam received us in the way of cities that have been looked at too long and have learned to hold the attention without trying — canals, bicycles, the particular angle of the light off the water in the afternoon. We dropped our bags at the flat — first floor, windows over the Bloemgracht, the canal right there below the sill as promised — and walked immediately, because that is what you do when you arrive somewhere and want to know if the city still fits.

It fit. Or almost.

I knew this city. I had lived here for four years, had cycled these streets and argued with their geography and finally learned them the way you learn a language — past the point of effort, into the thing itself. Standing at the corner of the Bloemgracht and looking east toward the Jordaan, I should have felt the ease of return. And I did, mostly. The Westerkerk was where it was supposed to be. The canal moved. The smell was right.

Mostly.

---

My first interview was on the Tuesday, at the city's digital office on Amstel 1 — a neat, institutional room, a civil servant named De Vries who was thoughtful about algorithmic accountability in the specific way of people who have been made responsible for a difficult thing and have decided to take it seriously. He talked for an hour and a half. I filled most of a notebook. Jonas was somewhere in the Jordaan with his bicycle and his own agenda, which suited us both.

The Waag meeting was on Thursday. Leonie, a researcher in her mid-forties, was better: less careful, more willing to say the uncomfortable thing about where algorithmic transparency actually broke down, which was at the point where the humans responsible for the systems decided they preferred not to know. I underlined several things. We talked for two and a half hours. I came back to the Bloemgracht in the late afternoon with the piece assembled in my head and a feeling of quiet satisfaction that I have learned to trust.

Jonas had cooked. I told him about Leonie.

---

We walked each evening. This was his idea, or became it — after dinner, along the canals, no particular route. Amsterdam does this to people; there is always another bridge, another canal, another thing to walk past. I navigated from memory, which mostly held.

On the Wednesday we went looking for the bookshop I had mentioned to Jonas in Berlin. The Athenaeum, on the Spui — and there it was, on the corner, unmistakable. I felt a brief and disproportionate relief. Jonas wanted to go in. We stayed for forty minutes and he bought two things he probably didn't need, which felt right.

Afterward he asked me to show him the route I used to walk from my student flat to the university. I took us south from the Spui along the Singel, which was correct, and then turned where the canal bends — and stopped.

"What?" Jonas said.

"I thought this connected further along," I said. I looked at the bridge, the turning, the way the canal went. "I must have usually come from the other direction."

He accepted this without interest and we went another way. I let the moment pass. It had been more than a year, and cities shift in memory even when they don't move. This was a reasonable explanation.

I gave it to myself and we walked on.

---

On the Friday, the last full day, I cooked.

The flat had a real kitchen, which I had been wanting to use all week. Jonas sat at the counter with a glass of wine and the particular quality of attention he has when he is watching me do something practical — not interference, just interest, the way he watches a problem being solved. I made *stamppot* because I had wanted it since landing and because the ingredients were available and because it felt right in a way I did not examine.

I was moving quickly, the knife finding its rhythm. This is something I have always been able to do — cooking with a speed that Jonas finds remarkable, though I have explained it as habit, as Oma Ingrid, as years of cooking alone with no reason to be slow.

I was cutting the kale — fast, rolling cut, knuckles curled — when the knife caught the tip of my left index finger.

It was brief. I pulled my hand back and held it over the sink, which is what you do. Jonas stood up immediately.

"I'm fine," I said.

"Let me—"

"It's fine. Sit down."

I ran the water over it. The sting arrived on schedule, which was reassuring. I pressed a cloth to it, applied pressure, waited. When Jonas came to look I was already composed.

"Barely anything," I said, and showed him — a small clean cut, already slowing. He went to find a plaster. I covered the finger and went back to the kale.

We ate at the table by the canal-side window, the evening light going, Jonas telling me about a conversation he'd had that morning with the owner of a print shop on the Elandsgracht — a man who had opinions about paper weights that Jonas had found unexpectedly interesting. I listened. I laughed where it was funny. I answered when he asked me something. The plaster on my finger was present but ordinary. The *stamppot* was good. We poured more wine and watched the light go off the water.

---

I woke at some point in the early hours. Jonas was asleep. The canal outside was dark and quiet, the kind of city quiet that is never entirely still.

I lay there for a moment, aware of my hand. The plaster. Something about it had been at the edge of my attention all evening without becoming anything specific.

I got up carefully and went to the bathroom, pulling the door almost closed so the light wouldn't wake him.

In the mirror: my face, familiar. My hand, raised.

I peeled the plaster back slowly.

The skin was closed. Not scabbed, not raw — closed, as if the cut had happened days ago and not four hours. The line where the knife had been was faint, already smoothing. I pressed the pad of my thumb against it. No tenderness. No heat. Nothing that said *injury* in any of the registers I knew to listen for.

I stood there for a long moment looking at my finger.

Too perfect, I thought. Too — efficient.

I replaced the plaster, turned off the light, and went back to bed.
