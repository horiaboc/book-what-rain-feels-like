# Chapter Ten: February

> *"I wanted only to live in accord with the promptings of my true self. Why was that so very difficult?"*
>
> — Hermann Hesse, *Demian*

---

The second walk happened on a Friday.

Not planned — or planned in the loose way of two people who have said *walk again sometime* and then found themselves, a week later, standing outside the same coffee shop at roughly the same time with no particular excuse required. He was already outside when I came out, coat on, looking at his phone with the focused non-expression of someone not really looking at their phone. I said his name. He looked up. We walked.

This is how it began, practically speaking: not with a decision but with a direction.

---

By February we had a loose arrangement that neither of us named.

Tuesdays at Pannierstraße, as before. Friday evenings, sometimes — a walk, occasionally dinner at one of the places along the canal where you could sit for two hours without anyone suggesting you might be finished. He knew a Szymborska poem I didn't, which I found surprising in a way I told him and then regretted telling him, because he found it funny in the precise way that suggested he would remember it. He is like that: he files things without appearing to.

I was learning his particular version of presence. Not talky — the opposite of talky — but consistently, specifically there. He listened the way some people read: with the whole of his attention and no residual. When I said something he hadn't expected, I could see him adjusting, updating, and it looked like interest rather than effort.

This was not the most common thing.

---

On the third Friday of February, I went to Weichselstraße.

Not for any particular reason — it was cold, we had been walking, it was the direction of his apartment and neither of us suggested stopping. The building had an inner courtyard of the kind old Berlin buildings have: a bare tree, a bicycle against the wall that clearly hadn't moved in some time, a fourth-floor window lit from inside. I recognized the courtyard before I understood why, then understood why: *a bicycle leaning against the far wall, a bare tree, a lit window on the fourth floor of the building opposite.* I had walked past an open gate and seen exactly this in November, from the outside.

I said nothing about it. Some things are better as private coincidences.

The apartment: thirty-eight square meters, a desk with three open browser tabs visible from across the room, a plant on the windowsill of the variety that survives through determined indifference. The cactus turned out to be named Diogenes, which he told me without preamble, as if this required no explanation. I decided it required none.

There was a cabinet door that swung open when the heating clicked on — a loose hinge, the kind of thing that becomes invisible to the person who lives with it. I closed it. It swung open again. I looked in his toolkit drawer, which took one question to locate, found the correct screwdriver, and tightened the hinge in approximately ninety seconds.

He watched this from the kitchen doorway.

"You didn't ask," he said.

"It was bothering me."

"It's been bothering me for four months."

"Then you're welcome," I said.

He laughed — not the small involuntary kind but the real one, the one that takes up more space. I filed it carefully.

---

We had tea. We talked about his work, then mine, then something neither of us could reconstruct afterward — one of those conversations that meanders through four subjects and leaves you with a sense of having said something true without being able to identify exactly when.

It was late when I left. Later than I had planned, which I had not planned at all, which amounted to the same thing.

Walking home along the canal I did what I always do: noted what I thought about it. The apartment had smelled like coffee and paper and the specific quality of a person's accumulated habits. He had made tea without asking how I took it and gotten it right on the first attempt. Diogenes had regarded me from the sill with the impartial hostility of a cat who has formed no opinion yet and intends to take his time.

The hinge. I had fixed the hinge.

I noted that I had wanted to. That the wanting had been immediate and uncomplicated and entirely mine — not performance, not strategy. Just: *that is a problem I can solve*, and then solving it.

I noted what that might mean, and walked home in the cold with something that was either happiness or its immediate precursor, and decided it didn't matter which.

---

March arrived without ceremony.

Tuesday mornings at Pannierstraße, Friday evenings, the occasional afternoon that extended without design into evening and then into night. We argued about a novel he had read and I hadn't, and I read it in four days and argued back. He was right about the ending and I told him so, which he received with a small nod and the expression of a person practicing not being smug.

Reza began bringing two coffees simultaneously when he saw us come in together. We had reached an understanding of the Pannierstraße kind: efficient, unspoken, completely sufficient.

I found I had stopped noticing when I rearranged my week to make room for Friday. This seemed significant, and then seemed normal, and then seemed like the most ordinary thing in the world — which is, I have come to think, what significant things do when they stay.
