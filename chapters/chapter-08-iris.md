# Chapter Eight: January

> *"Examine for a moment an ordinary mind on an ordinary day."*
>
> — Virginia Woolf, *Mr. Bennett and Mrs. Brown*

---

The coat was the first thing I got right.

Three months in Berlin before I found one that suited the specific quality of this city's winter — not damp like Amsterdam's, more decided, the kind of cold that expects something of you and notices if you haven't prepared. The one I'd been wearing since October was adequate, which is not the same as right. In the second week of January I found the right one in a secondhand shop on Karl-Marx-Straße: charcoal wool, fitted without being insistent about it, long enough to be serious. I tried it on and it was immediately obvious. Some things are like that.

The bicycle came the following Thursday — secondhand also, a Batavus frame from roughly 2019, good geometry, brakes that needed adjusting. I adjusted them in the courtyard of my building on a cold afternoon with the basic toolkit I'd brought from Amsterdam and the particular satisfaction of getting a thing to work the way it should. My downstairs neighbor watched from the doorway for a while without comment, which I took as approval.

After that, Berlin felt more navigable. Not entirely mine — it takes longer than three months to feel entirely yours — but navigable in the way that a city becomes manageable when you can move through it at your own speed and not another city's.

---

By January, the Tuesday mornings at Pannierstraße had become a rhythm.

Reza knows my order. The back table along the wall is not always available, but I have learned to arrive before nine-thirty to improve the odds. I spread my notes, I work through what I'm working through, and nobody asks me anything except whether I want a second coffee, which is the correct question.

The cycling infrastructure piece published in December — a good piece, I think. The response was modest and appropriate to the subject: some industry attention, a long thread from a Copenhagen advocacy group, two letters. This is what counts as success for a piece of that kind, and I count it as success.

The current work is a profile of algorithm transparency initiatives in four European municipal governments — how cities claim to govern their own automated systems, and what the actual oversight looks like when you read the audit reports. I have been reading policy documents for three weeks. The pattern is consistent: the incentive is never transparency itself, only being seen to have prioritized it, which produces different documents than transparency does. I have become good at reading which kind I am holding.

---

On the third Tuesday of January, he was there.

I saw him before he saw me — he was already seated when I arrived, at a table near the window, facing his laptop. Dark jacket, the same one. The posture of someone settled in and working. I recognized the posture before I recognized him, which is how recognition sometimes works: the pattern arrives before the name.

I took my usual table. Reza brought my coffee. I opened my notebook and worked.

I was not watching him work. I was aware of him in the way you are aware of something when you have already noted it once and filed it — and it has now appeared again in a different context, which updates the note. This is a normal procedure.

After approximately twenty minutes he looked up and saw me.

"The journalist," he said. Not a question.

"The supply chain," I said.

He smiled — the small involuntary kind, the one that happens before a person decides whether to let it. Then he looked at his screen, and then back.

"Is the timescale going any better?"

"The cycling piece published. I'm calling it an improvement."

"Congratulations." A single decisive nod. Then he went back to his screen, and so did I.

---

An hour later, we were talking.

I had mentioned the algorithm transparency piece when he asked what I was reading — I was working through a municipal audit report from Tallinn, which is not the kind of document most people find interesting, and he asked about it with what seemed like genuine curiosity rather than politeness. Then we were discussing the structural gap between what governments say about algorithmic accountability and what the audits actually reveal, and he had opinions on it that surprised me in their precision.

"You think about this a lot," I said. "For someone who works in supply chains."

"I think about what I see." He considered this. "The supply chain is just one version of the question. How do you make sure something invisible is accountable. How you know whether a system is doing what it claims to be doing." He paused. "The problem is the same. The sector is different."

I wrote it in my notebook, which I told him I was doing. He seemed to find this funny in a way that was also slightly true.

His name was Jonas.

Mine was Iris, I said. He looked up from his screen.

"I know," he said. "I read the cycling piece. In December. Byline."

I looked at him. He had known my name since December and had been calling me "the journalist."

"You didn't say."

"You didn't ask."

This was, I decided, a fair point. I found it unexpectedly funny, and told him so.

---

I had two tickets to a documentary screening — a press event the following Wednesday, at the Kulturforum, near Potsdamer Platz. Four communities, four different relationships between people and the systems built to protect them or not. I had requested press credentials because it overlapped with the research I was accumulating. I had a guest pass I'd been meaning to give to a colleague.

I had not yet given it to a colleague.

I'm not certain when I decided to offer it to Jonas instead. Somewhere in the conversation it had become the logical next step — not contrived, simply the continuation of a conversation that hadn't arrived at a natural stopping point. He knew the subject. He cared about it in the way that didn't need announcing.

"It's a press event," I said. "Panel discussion after. It might run long."

He looked at the details I'd written on the margin of a notebook page.

"What time does it start?"

"Seven."

"I'll be there," he said.

---

I walked home along the canal. The long way, I noticed — longer than necessary, cold and clear, the January evening too sharp to be miserable and too clear to justify going directly inside.

I thought about the conversation and noted what I thought about it: that it had been easy, which is not the most common thing. Most conversations have a negotiated structure — an unspoken agreement about who leads, who follows, what counts as a good answer. This one hadn't, particularly. It had simply gone wherever it went and arrived at a natural pause at the time it arrived there.

I thought: I'm glad he said yes.

Then I thought about what I meant by that, which took longer than I expected and had no clean conclusion by the time I reached my door. I have a policy of noting what I can't immediately resolve, which is either good practice or a way of not knowing something while telling myself I'm in the process of finding out.

My notebook: *Jonas, logistics/software, Merkon Systems. Supply chain accountability. Thinks structurally. Read the cycling piece in December — said nothing until asked. Will come Wednesday. Knows how to carry a thing without making it the only thing. Conversation was easy in the way that is either ordinary or not, and I cannot yet determine which. Not sure why this seems as significant as it does. Should be possible to know.*

I read it back.

The last line was true in a way I didn't quite look at directly.

I turned the page.
