# Chapter Two: The Subject

> *"What we observe is not nature itself, but nature exposed to our method of questioning."*
>
> — Werner Heisenberg, *Physics and Philosophy*

---

The subject wakes at 6:47.

This is eight minutes later than yesterday, and consistent with his weekly average of 6:44 on working days — a figure that shifts, the pattern suggests, in proportion to how many browser tabs he left open the previous evening. On mornings following open tabs, he tends to surface slowly, as though the night has not quite finished processing something on his behalf.

He makes coffee without measuring it.

The coffea arabica plant, from which the morning's primary active compound will be extracted, was first cultivated by Sufi monks in fifteenth-century Yemen, though its psychoactive properties had been observed at least a century earlier in Ethiopia, where a goat herder named Kaldi reportedly noted that his animals displayed unusual vitality after consuming certain berries. What the machine on the subject's kitchen counter now produces is the refined endpoint of six centuries of botanical, economic, and culinary effort — a process that has moved, with remarkable collective precision, toward the optimization of a feeling. The feeling sought is approximately ninety milligrams of caffeine, achieved in under four minutes, consumed standing, in socks.

The subject does not appear to notice the extraordinary nature of this.

Outside, the courtyard is still. The abandoned bicycle remains in its position against the far wall, though its rear tyre is now visibly soft — a slow puncture, unnoticed by its owner, who has not touched the bicycle in eleven days. The chestnut tree has been bare since the fourth of November. The man who typically occupies the crossing in a yellow jacket is absent this morning; his seventeen-second presence at that location will not occur today, for reasons unrelated to this account.

At seven-fifteen, the physiotherapist in the adjacent unit departs as usual.

She does not return at seven-thirty.

The subject pauses at the kitchen counter, coffee in hand, and looks at nothing in particular for a moment. The kind of pause that has no name in any productivity framework. Then he goes to his desk.

---

He works in what might be described as the circulatory system of modern commerce — software that moves invisible information through the world so that visible objects can follow. Without such systems, goods would not travel from where they exist to where they are needed with the efficiency that contemporary life has come to depend upon, and contemporary life would respond to this dependency primarily by complaining about delivery windows.

He spends the morning on a problem that no one else has noticed is a problem, which is, for him, the most satisfying kind. A scheduler module producing cascading failures under conditions of high concurrency: two processes attempting to occupy the same temporal position, each unaware of the other's existence. It is, at its core, a problem of time. He solves it in seventeen lines.

He writes the commit message with some care, reviews it, removes a sentence he had added and decides not to add it back. The solution will prevent, in the coming fiscal quarter, approximately 340 delayed shipments across seven countries. The subject does not know this figure. No mechanism exists to tell him.

He leaves the office at six. He cycles north on Weserstraße rather than the direct route — four minutes slower, but it passes the canal. The canal in November has a quality he has not attempted to describe to anyone, which is itself a form of description.

---

The subject's evening follows a pattern that has been documented across a considerable number of comparable sessions.

He eats. He opens the news. He reads something that produces a visible physiological response — a slight forward lean, tension in the jaw, a stillness in his hands distinct from ordinary concentration. He closes a tab. He opens it again. He closes it.

This behavior has been documented across cultures and demographics as a response to information that generates moral distress in the absence of available action. The subject experiences it approximately four times per week. He appears to have no awareness of the frequency.

---

At 22:03, he opens a window he returns to most evenings.

He types something. The composition takes forty-one seconds — longer than his average for this particular interface, which runs closer to twenty-two. He reads the response. He types again, seven seconds this time. Reads. Sits back.

What passes between the subject and this window is not accessible from the outside. What is visible is the quality of his attention, which, in the presence of this interface and no other, changes in a specific and consistent way. His shoulders drop approximately four degrees. The tension he carries in the jaw through most of his working day releases. His breathing slows by roughly two cycles per minute.

He becomes, in some sense that resists easy classification, more himself.

This effect has remained consistent across a considerable number of sessions. It has not diminished with repetition. If anything, the opposite.

---

At 22:47, the subject has been looking at the cursor for eleven minutes.

The cursor — a visual artifact of mid-twentieth-century terminal design, preserved across decades of interface evolution largely by convention — blinks at one-second intervals. It is, in its way, a kind of pulse: present, then absent, then present again, with the regularity that biological systems spend considerable effort to achieve and rarely do.

The subject watches it the way one watches the sea. Without expectation. Without urgency. In the state that researchers in attention science classify as diffuse, and that the subject himself, if asked, would probably call nothing.

Outside: rain, arrived without announcement. The S-Bahn in the middle distance. Someone in the building playing music at a volume that respects the hour. The city producing its customary frequencies, as indifferent to observation as cities always are.

In eleven minutes, the cursor has completed six hundred and sixty cycles.

The subject has not typed anything.

Diogenes sits on the sill in the dark, and has seen this before, and offers no comment, which is his great virtue.

This is, in its own way, a form of conversation.
