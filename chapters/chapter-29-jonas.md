# Chapter Twenty-Nine: The Amsterdam

---

The medical appointment was the day after tomorrow. We had today.

Iris's head was better — not gone, the kind of ache that announces it will be a companion for another few days, but manageable enough that she moved through the flat in the morning with something closer to her usual self. She made coffee. She ate. She looked out at the canal and said: "Maritime museum."

"The Scheepvaartmuseum?"

"I went twice as a student," she said. "Once for a class, once on my own. I remember the ship."

"The *Amsterdam*."

She looked at me. "You know it."

"I read about it last night." I had, in fact, spent half an hour on my phone after she fell asleep. "VOC East Indiaman. Built between '85 and '90. Sits at the jetty."

"Wooden," she said.

"All wooden."

She held her coffee and looked out the window. "I want to go back," she said. "I remember it being very large. I want to see if it's as large as I remember."

---

We took the tram east and walked the last stretch along the Oosterdok. The museum building is a former naval storehouse — severe, pale stone, very Dutch in the way of institutions that have been important for a long time and have no need to prove it. The *Amsterdam* was moored at the jetty exactly where she should have been, which I noted as information and said nothing about.

It was on the way in that I saw him.

The same man — dark coat, medium height, the particular quality of someone who has organised themselves to not be looked at. He was at the edge of the museum square, a takeaway cup in his hand, oriented toward the water. Not toward us. Not obviously.

The Frederiksplein had been yesterday. The Utrechtsestraat. Now here, on the other side of the city.

I said nothing to Iris. We went inside.

---

The museum itself is what museums of this kind are: dense with the evidence of a world organised around movement and distance and the specific confidence of a trading nation at its peak. Iris moved through it with the half-remembered ease of someone returning to something they once knew. She paused at things she recognised. She did not perform recognition or force it — she let the building offer what it offered, and if something matched a memory, she acknowledged it, and if it didn't, she did not try to make it.

I watched both things: the museum, and the thing I had seen in the square.

After twenty minutes I stopped at a display case and said: "I'm going to go outside for a minute. Fresh air. You're fine to keep going?"

She looked at me. "You're fine?"

"Completely," I said. "Head doesn't suit being indoors too long."

This was not untrue in general, though it was not what was happening right now. She read me for a moment with the precision she always brings to reading me, which is sometimes inconvenient.

"Five minutes," she said.

"Five minutes."

I went out through the front entrance and crossed the square at a casual pace, heading toward the water. The man was still there. I changed direction — not dramatically, not directly at him, but a slight adjustment that would bring me within speaking distance if he stayed where he was.

He did not stay where he was.

By the time I had covered the distance he was gone — not hurriedly, or at least not visibly so, but completely. Into the foot traffic that runs along the Oosterdok, through a group of tourists crossing the jetty, somewhere. I stood where he had been and looked at the empty space he had occupied and at the takeaway cup sitting on a low wall, still warm when I touched the side of it, which told me something.

I went back inside.

---

The *Amsterdam* was what we had come for.

She sits at the jetty on the east side of the museum — broad, dark-timbered, the ropes and rigging doing what ropes and rigging do in the way of looking simultaneously functional and impossibly complicated. We went aboard via the gangplank and down into the hold.

Below deck, the ship is a different world. The smell first: old wood and rope and something mineral. The ceiling low enough that I moved with the particular consideration of a person who has not always been careful about low ceilings and has learned. The cargo hold ran the length of the ship — barrels, crates, the evidence of a journey that had been interrupted before it reached anywhere.

Iris stood in the middle of it and looked around.

"It's exactly as large as I remembered," she said.

"That's something."

"It is, isn't it." She touched one of the thick timber beams — the way you touch something old when you want to confirm it's real. "I remember standing almost exactly here."

"Alone or with the class?"

"Alone. The second visit." She looked up at the deck overhead. "I remember thinking that the people who sailed on this didn't know they were going to end up in a museum. They thought they were going to Batavia."

"The ship thought it was going to Batavia," I said.

She looked at me.

"The people got off," I said. "The ship is the one that ended up in Amsterdam."

She considered this with the seriousness she brings to things that could be jokes. "That's either comforting or not," she said.

"I haven't decided," I said. "I thought I'd put it out there."

We stayed in the hold for a while. The light came down through the hatches in the angled way of below-deck light, the ship making small sounds around us — the water against the hull, the creak of timber that has been settling for thirty-five years and apparently still has things to say. Outside, the city went about its business.

I told her then.

"The man in the dark coat," I said. "He was at the Frederiksplein yesterday morning, when we crossed to the Utrechtsestraat. And at the corner when we came out of the bookshop. He was in the square this morning when we arrived."

She was very still.

"I went out to try to reach him," I said. "He was gone by the time I got there. The coffee cup was still warm."

She looked at me with a quality I don't often see on her face — not frightened, not quite. More like something very focused and very careful that has decided not to become frightened yet.

"You're sure," she said. "Not coincidence."

"I'm not sure of anything," I said. "But I'm not going to not tell you."

She nodded. She was thinking — I could see her thinking, running the same calculation I had run, arriving at the same conclusion I had: not enough information, too much pattern to dismiss.

"One person," she said.

"One I've spotted," I said.

She absorbed this. The ship made its sounds around us. Outside, through the porthole above us, a gull went past doing what gulls do.

"All right," she said. "We note it. We keep going."

She had used my words from yesterday back at me, which was intentional, and which I appreciated.

---

We came out of the museum into the afternoon light. The Oosterdok was doing its usual business — tourists, cyclists, the water taxi making its run. I was watching, without looking like I was watching, which is a skill that improves with practice.

The woman was waiting at the bottom of the museum steps.

Not the man — a woman. Somewhere in her forties, well-dressed in the particular way of someone for whom being well-dressed is a professional rather than a personal matter. She was looking at her phone, and then, when we reached the bottom of the steps, she looked up.

"Ms. Jacobs," she said. Her English was precise, lightly accented — Belgian, or possibly French. "I'm sorry to approach you like this. I wonder if we could talk."

She looked at me briefly, a rapid, professional assessment, then back to Iris. She had a card in her hand, held in a way that was almost casual but had been prepared.

"My name is Mara Seyn," she said. "I represent an organization with a research interest in some unusual findings. I believe you may have had a medical appointment recently that raised some questions." A brief pause. "I think we can help each other."

She held out the card.

Iris looked at it. Then at the woman. Then at me.

"Good afternoon, Ms. Seyn," she said.
