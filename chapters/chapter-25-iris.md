# Chapter Twenty-Five: OLVG

---

The ER triage nurse asked me to rate my pain on a scale of one to ten. I said four, which was accurate. She wrote it down without looking up. Jonas was at the desk behind me filling in a form; I could hear him asking how to spell my surname in Dutch and then deciding it didn't matter.

My shoulder hurt. The graze on my forearm had been cleaned and dressed by a nurse who worked fast and didn't ask unnecessary questions, which I appreciated. The shoulder was the concern — impact, possible fracture, they said, we'll need to image it. I said of course.

I was calm. I noted that I was calm. It seemed the appropriate response: I had fallen from a bicycle, I was at a hospital, they were going to take an X-ray. This was a sequence with a clear logic and a clear resolution.

---

The X-ray took eight minutes. The technician positioned my arm with practiced precision, told me not to breathe, took the image, told me I could breathe.

I waited in the curtained bay. Jonas sat in the plastic chair beside me. He had bought two coffees from a machine in the corridor — bad ones, he reported — and he handed me mine and I held it because my hands needed something to do.

He told me about the bicycle shop man, whose name he had gotten at some point during the taxi confusion: Pieter. Pieter had offered to hold our bikes until we could collect them, which Jonas thought was kind.

"Very Amsterdam," I said.

"Very Amsterdam," he agreed.

We drank the bad coffee. I watched the gap in the curtain where the corridor was visible — shoes going past, the bottom of a nurse's uniform, a trolley wheel — and I thought about nothing in particular, which is what I do when I am waiting for something and already know it is coming.

---

The radiologist came back sooner than I expected.

He was a man in his fifties, with the manner of someone who has delivered difficult information many times and has a practiced way of being careful with it. He introduced himself — Van Houten — and said he wanted to discuss the imaging with me and the attending physician together, if that was all right.

I said of course.

Jonas looked at me. I didn't look back.

Dr. Van Houten disappeared and came back with a younger doctor — the attending — and they arranged themselves on the far side of the bay with the slightly self-conscious formality of people who are about to say something they have not said before and are not sure of the right register.

Van Houten said: the X-ray of the shoulder showed no fracture, no dislocation, the joint was structurally intact, she would need rest and anti-inflammatories.

I said good.

He said: however. He said it carefully, as a sentence by itself.

The bone density in the imaging was unusual. Not concerning in the clinical sense — there was nothing pathological, nothing that suggested disease or injury. But the density profile was outside what they typically saw. Outside, he said, what they had ever seen, and he said this with a precision that I understood was not exaggeration. He said they would like to do an MRI.

"To understand it better," the attending said.

"Of course," I said.

Jonas's hand found mine under the blanket. I held it. My face was doing exactly what I needed it to do, which is to say: interested, slightly concerned in the way anyone would be, cooperative. I have always been good at faces.

Inside: something different. Something that was not quite fear because I don't experience fear quite the way the word suggests — but a version of it. A sharpening. The thing that has been sitting at the back of my thinking since the bathroom on the Bloemgracht, since the plaster, since the seamless skin, began to move toward the front.

I said: of course. Let's do the MRI.

---

The MRI machine is loud in a specific rhythmic way — a series of knocking pulses, each burst slightly different, like a very large piece of machinery spelling something out in a code it will not translate. I lay inside it for forty-seven minutes. I know this because I counted the sequences.

My shoulder ached. The graze on my forearm was itching, which I took as ordinary healing. I concentrated on keeping still.

What I thought about: the street in the Jordaan that had been different. The building near the Leidseplein that I had described to Jonas once — the red façade, the stepped gable — and had not been able to find when we walked past on Wednesday. The turning on the canal route I thought I knew and did not. The coffee shop at De Correspondent that my colleague had described as being on the opposite side of the building from where I had placed it.

Small things. Individually, each one was nothing.

I lay inside the machine and it knocked its code and I thought: four things. Four separate small failures of a memory that was supposed to hold.

Then I thought: and the finger. And what was under the plaster.

The machine ran its final sequence. The noise stopped. The bed slid out.

---

They kept us waiting for an hour and a half.

Jonas got more coffee. He told me about a book he'd been reading before Amsterdam — something about the history of Amsterdam's canal system, which he'd bought from the Athenaeum on our Wednesday visit. He talked about the engineering of it, the seventeenth century, the way the city had built itself into a swamp and made it hold. He was doing what he does when he's worried: filling the space with information, staying close, not asking the question.

I listened. I was glad of the sound of his voice.

Van Houten came back with the attending and, this time, a third doctor — a woman, older, who was introduced as a specialist in radiology. Her name was Maes. She was the one who had been brought in to look at the images, I understood, and then someone else had been brought in after her, and then possibly someone after that, but Maes was the one who came in to the room.

She sat down, which the others had not done.

She said: the bone imaging showed an anomalous structural pattern that they had not been able to classify. Anomalous in density — far outside normal range for human bone, in the direction of significantly greater density — and anomalous in uniformity. Normal bone has variation — a spongy inner structure, gradients, biological irregularity. Her bone showed none of this. It was more consistent with — she paused — a manufactured material than with biological bone growth. She said this very carefully and watched me as she said it.

She said: bone marrow was present and appeared functional. Blood work was normal. There were no clinical anomalies in any of the other standard metrics.

She said: there was one other finding. In the brain scan — and here she paused again — there was an atypical pattern in the cortical region. Not in the rest of the brain. Only the cortex. The pattern was anomalous in the same direction as the bone: not pathological, not concerning in the clinical sense, but beyond the range of variation they could account for. Enhanced connectivity, she said. A microstructural pattern that was simply not in their reference data.

She said: and there was a small object near the collarbone. Not metallic in the standard classification. Biologically encased. They were not sure what it was.

Then she stopped and waited.

I thought: she is waiting to see what I will say. She thinks I might know something. She is right that I might know something. I don't know what I know.

I said: I don't understand what this means.

She said: neither do we. Not yet. She said they were going to document the findings and she wanted to refer me to a specialist. She said: are you in pain? I said: my shoulder, a little. She said they would prescribe something for that.

I said: are you keeping me here?

She said: no. There was no clinical reason to keep me. But she would like to follow up.

I said: of course.

Jonas had not spoken. I looked at him, finally, and his face was the face of someone running a calculation he doesn't yet have all the inputs for. Not fear. Not rejection. Just: a problem whose shape he can see and whose solution he cannot yet read.

He looked back at me. He reached over and tucked a piece of hair behind my ear, which he has done before in moments when there is nothing to say and the gesture says it instead.

I thought: whatever is coming, he is going to stay. I don't know how I know this. I know it.

Dr. Maes gave us her card. She said to call in the morning. She said to rest.

We walked out into the Amsterdam evening, the hospital door sliding shut behind us, the canal smell coming in on the air — and neither of us spoke, but his shoulder was next to mine and the city was still going and the light on the water was the same light it had been that morning, which was a strange comfort.
