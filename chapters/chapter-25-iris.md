# Chapter Twenty-Five: OLVG

---

I came back in pieces.

First: sound. A steady electronic tone somewhere to my left. The particular ambient hum of a building that never fully stops. Voices in a corridor, not close.

Then: the ceiling. White panels. A curtain on a rail, pale green, half-drawn.

Then: Jonas.

He was in the chair beside the bed, leaning forward with his elbows on his knees, watching me with the careful stillness of someone who has been sitting that way for some time. When he saw my eyes open, something in his face that had been held very tight loosened.

"Hi," he said.

I took inventory. My head ached — a dull insistent weight on the left side, above the ear. A dressing, I could feel it. My right forearm was bandaged. I was in a hospital bed with a monitor clipped to my finger and an IV in my left hand.

"I fell off a bike," I said.

He confirmed this. We talked. I tried to sit up, which was inadvisable. He told me the doctors wanted to do some imaging to make sure there was nothing wrong inside my head and I said of course, and meant it, and lay back, and let the ceiling be the ceiling for a while.

---

They took me for the scan within the hour.

The machine was loud. I lay inside it with my eyes closed and tracked the sequences by sound — different tones for different things, the bed moving slightly between them. I concentrated on keeping still, which is easier than it might seem when your head hurts and movement makes it worse.

What I thought about: the wall at the end of the street. The fountain that wasn't there. The specific quality of that moment — not the wrongness of it, which I had processed and set aside, but the way I had set it aside. Automatically. As if setting things aside were a trained reflex.

The machine made its last sound. The bed slid out.

---

They kept us waiting for an hour and a half.

Jonas got coffee from somewhere and told me about the Amsterdam canal system — its construction, the seventeenth century, the way the city had built itself into a swamp and made it hold. He had been reading about it before we came and had opinions. I listened and was glad of his voice.

The doctor who came in was a woman in her fifties — Dr. Maes, she said, radiology — and she brought the attending with her and sat down, which I noticed.

She said: the head scan was clear. No bleeding, no swelling, no structural injury. I had a concussion and would need to rest and be monitored for the next forty-eight hours, but there was nothing immediately dangerous.

I said good.

She said: however.

She said the imaging had shown some findings she wanted to discuss. She was careful with her language — not alarming, deliberately not alarming, the register of someone choosing each word as she went. She said: the bone structure in the areas we had imaged was not typical. It was denser than normal, considerably denser, and with an unusual degree of uniformity. Bone is normally irregular, she said — it varies, it has a particular kind of inner scaffolding. Hers was different. Not unhealthy. Simply unlike anything in their reference data.

She said: there was also a small area near the collarbone, visible in the imaging, which she could not classify. It did not appear to be anything pathological. But it was present, and she wanted to note it.

I said: what does it mean?

She said: she didn't know yet. It could indicate a genetic variation, something inherited that simply hadn't been identified before. It was also possible there was a condition they weren't familiar with. She wanted to refer me to a specialist.

She said: the brain scan also showed something atypical. Not in the brain as a whole — the scan was otherwise clean. But the cortex, the outer layer, showed a pattern of activity and structure that was outside normal range. Higher connectivity than expected, an unusual arrangement. She was not sure how to interpret it. She said: are you aware of any history of neurological conditions? Any unusual symptoms?

I said: no.

She said: do you ever experience things that feel — and here she paused again — unusually vivid? Memories that seem too clear, too detailed?

I looked at her.

I said: sometimes.

She nodded as if I had confirmed something she had only been guessing at. She said they would like to follow up. She said: and the grazes on your arm — the nurse noted that they seem to be healing quite quickly. More quickly than she would usually expect.

She said this without emphasis, the way you mention something you have written down but are not sure what to do with.

I said: I heal fast. I always have.

She nodded again. She gave me her card. She said to rest. She said to call if anything changed.

---

Jonas had not spoken through any of it.

I looked at him, finally, when Maes and the attending had gone. He was sitting forward in the chair, elbows on his knees, looking at the floor. Then he looked up at me.

His face was doing something I had not seen on it before. Something that was not composure and not fear but something in between — the look of someone who has received information they haven't yet found a place to put.

He reached over and tucked a piece of hair behind my ear. He has done this before in moments where the gesture has to do what the words can't.

"She said it's not dangerous," I offered.

"I heard," he said.

"The head is fine."

"I know."

He sat back. He looked at me for a long moment.

"Are you in pain?"

"My head," I said. "Less than before."

He nodded. He looked at his hands, then back at me. "We're going to figure it out," he said. Not a question. Not quite a promise either — more like a fact he had already decided on.

I said: "I know."

We walked out into the Amsterdam evening when they discharged me. The hospital door slid shut behind us. The canal smell came in on the air. His shoulder was next to mine and the city was still doing what it does, the light on the water the same light it had been that morning, indifferent to what had happened inside.

I was glad of the indifference. It made the city feel real.
