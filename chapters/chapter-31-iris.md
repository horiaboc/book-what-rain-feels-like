# Chapter Thirty-One: Zandvoort

---

We took the train to Zandvoort on the Thursday.

It is thirty minutes from Amsterdam Centraal on a direct line — a small seaside town on the North Sea coast, known for a racing circuit and for being the nearest beach to Amsterdam, which means on summer weekends it is overwhelmed and on autumn weekdays it is almost empty. We went on a weekday morning and the train was half-full: families with young children, an older couple with a thermos, a group of students going for reasons of their own.

I had been to Zandvoort in my second year at the journalism school — a day trip, a group of us, August, the end of the summer term. I remembered it clearly. The long walk from the station to the beach, the specific way the dunes give way to the sea, a café on the southern end of the promenade where we had spent most of the afternoon. The café was called — I reached for the name — *De Zeemeeuw*. The Seagull. Wooden terrace, blue chairs, a menu with the kind of fish soup that tastes of the coast. I had described it to Jonas once in Berlin when we were talking about Dutch seaside towns, and he had said it sounded good, and I had said: I'll show you.

Now I was going to show him. I was aware, boarding the train at Centraal, that I was doing this with a different kind of attention than I had brought to the journalism school. I was watching myself remember.

---

The dunes were right. They are hard to get wrong — a particular geography, a specific smell of salt grass and sand. The walk from the station was right: fifteen minutes, exactly, through the small town center and then the dunes opening up and the sea below.

The beach was almost empty, the way of northern European beaches in late August when the school year has started. A man walking a dog in the distance. The sea grey and particular, the kind of sea that doesn't perform.

We walked south along the promenade. I was watching for the café.

I found it in the right location — the southern end, as I remembered, where the promenade curves slightly. The building was there. The terrace was there, open but unoccupied, the chairs stacked against the tables in the way of a place expecting the season to end.

The chairs were red.

In my memory they are blue. I am certain of blue — the specific shade, the way the blue looked against the grey sea on that August afternoon, the way it looked in the photographs one of the students took and put on their social feed that evening. I had looked at those photographs later and thought: yes, that's exactly it.

Red chairs. A terrace arranged identically to the one I remembered, the same geometry, the same relationship to the promenade. But red.

Jonas said nothing. He had seen me stop.

I went inside.

The man at the counter was in his fifties. I asked him, in Dutch, how long the café had been here. He said since 1987 — his family's, his parents before him. I asked: have the chairs always been red? He looked at me as if this was a slightly unusual question. He said: always red, yes. His mother's choice. Red for the fish soup, she said; blue is cold.

I thanked him.

We sat down and ordered the soup.

---

"The chairs," Jonas said.

"I know."

"Same café. Same location. Wrong chairs."

"And the photographs," I said. "I was certain because of the photographs. I could see them in my memory — the chairs were blue in the photographs."

Jonas wrapped his hands around his bowl and looked out at the sea. "The photographs you remember."

"The photographs I remember seeing," I said. "That someone else took."

"Which may also be—"

"Constructed. Yes."

He was quiet for a moment. "This is actually a more informative failure," he said. "The other things — the entrance, the coffee machine — those could theoretically be explained. Changed, renovated, misremembered in the ordinary way. But this is a fixed, verifiable attribute. The chairs have always been red. Your memory of them is blue. And the photographs in your memory show blue."

"Yes."

"Someone made a mistake," he said. Very simply.

I thought about this. "A small mistake," I said. "A detail. Everything else about the café is correct."

"They got most of it right," he said. "They got the chairs wrong."

I looked out at the North Sea. The dog-walker had disappeared. The grey water moved against the shore with the indifferent rhythm of something that has been doing this for a very long time.

"How many people," I said, "would have to have been involved in — in constructing a past like mine. A believable one."

Jonas thought about it. "Not many," he said. "If you had access to the right systems, the right information. Public records, photographs, institutional databases, social history. Most of what people remember about their pasts is available somewhere. You'd need to do the research. You'd need to synthesize it."

"And get some chairs wrong."

"And get some chairs wrong," he said.

We ate the soup, which was good. The sea went on doing what it did.

---

It happened on the way back to the station.

We were walking through the town center — a pedestrian street, a few shops still open, an end-of-season quiet. There was a couple behind us, a woman and a man: the woman on the right, the man slightly behind her. I had not registered them on the beach walk. I registered them now because the woman had the specific attentive stillness of someone whose attention is not on what she appears to be looking at.

I said, quietly: "Don't turn around."

Jonas adjusted his stride very slightly — not a reaction, just an acknowledgment.

"Two people," I said. "Twenty metres back. The woman has been at the same distance since the café."

"Since before," he said. "I think she was on the promenade."

We were approaching an intersection. A souvenir shop on the right with racks outside. I said: "Stop at the rack." We stopped. I turned as if looking at tea towels, and looked instead at the street.

The couple had stopped too. The man was looking at something in his phone. The woman had turned slightly away.

They were too coordinated. Not conspicuously — but if you were looking for it, if you had the day Jonas had had on the Singel, it was there.

I put down the tea towel and said quietly: "We need to go faster."

We went faster.

The street opened into a small square and I took us left without explaining — I knew the square, correctly, the geography of this part of town my memory had got right — and then right, through a narrow passage between two buildings, and out onto a parallel street.

I stopped and listened.

No footsteps behind us.

Jonas was breathing slightly faster. He looked at me. "Where did you know about the passage?"

"Hm?"

"The passage between the buildings. You took it without hesitating."

I thought about it. "I don't know," I said. "I knew it was there."

He looked at me for a moment. Then he said: "All right."

We walked the rest of the way to the station at an ordinary pace, took the next train back to Amsterdam, and did not speak about what had happened until we were on the train and the coast had gone behind the dunes and the flat Dutch landscape was going past the window, ordinary and enormous.

I said: "I knew the passage."

"Yes," he said.

"I knew it even though nothing else about the town was entirely right."

"Yes."

"What does that mean?"

He was quiet for a moment. "It might mean you have — different kinds of memory. The spatial one, the body-knowing-where-to-go one — maybe that works differently from the detail memory. Maybe whatever was built into you built those two things differently."

I thought about that. The way I had gone through the passage: not remembered it, exactly. *Known* it. The way I know where things are in a kitchen. The way I know how a joint should sit before I tighten it.

The body knowing, rather than the mind.

"We'll keep going," Jonas said. "We'll find out what's different and what isn't."

Outside, the dunes gave way to flat fields. The train was warm. I leaned my head back against the seat and thought: someone gave me a memory of blue chairs. They worked very hard at it. They gave me the photographs. They gave me the afternoon. They just got the colour wrong.

I was not angry. I wasn't sure what I was, but it was not anger. It was something more like the feeling you get reading a letter that was written to you with care and contains a small, human error — the kind that proves someone was trying.
