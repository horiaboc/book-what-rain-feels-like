# Chapter Six: A Small Exchange

> *"It is only with the heart that one can see rightly; what is essential is invisible to the eye."*
>
> — Antoine de Saint-Exupéry, *The Little Prince*

---

The coffee shop on Pannierstraße has a television mounted in the corner that nobody asked for and nobody turns off.

I go there on Tuesday mornings because the tables along the back wall are wide enough to spread notes across, the coffee is good, and the owner — a compact man named Reza who moves with the energy of someone who has been awake since four — never asks if you want anything else once you have settled in. We have reached an understanding. I have been coming here for two weeks and I think we are, in the particular way of Berlin, becoming regulars to each other.

This Tuesday the television is showing a news segment. The sound is turned low — not off, but low enough that you catch only fragments if you aren't specifically listening. I am not specifically listening. I am working through notes for the cycling infrastructure piece, thinking about the gap between what the data says and what the cities are actually doing about it, which is where most policy stories live and where the interesting ones die or don't.

Then the image changes.

Flooding. A delta somewhere — I catch the chyron before looking away. Mekong. Families moving through brown water at mid-thigh, carrying children and the compressed weight of what they could take. The familiar architecture of these images: same camera angles, same combination of forward motion and exhaustion, same distance between the image and anything that might be done about it.

The man two tables away makes a sound.

Not quite a word. More like the beginning of one — an exhale with intention behind it, stopped before it arrived anywhere. I look up without deciding to.

He is looking at the screen. Thirty or so, dark jacket, a laptop open in front of him that he has stopped looking at. He has the expression of someone for whom this image is not new and has not gotten easier with repetition. Someone who has been carrying this specific weight for long enough that the carrying is no longer effort, just posture.

He notices me noticing.

"Same pattern," he says, quietly, as if completing a thought that hadn't been meant to leave him. "Different coordinates."

"Third time this season," I say.

He looks at me. A beat of recalibration — he hadn't expected an answer.

"You're following it."

"I write about it. Among other things." A pause. "Meridian."

He nods. Not at the outlet — I don't think he knows it — but at the category of person who would follow a flooding season across three news cycles. An acknowledgement of a type.

Then he looks back at the television, which has already moved to something else. A financial story. The Mekong is gone.

"Does it help?" he says, still looking at the screen. "Writing about it."

I consider this more carefully than the question perhaps warrants. It is a question I ask myself and have not finished answering.

"I think so," I say. "I'm still working out who it helps."

He seems to find this acceptable — or at least honest. He nods once, a small conclusive movement, and turns back to his laptop.

"Good luck with it," he says.

I say thank you and go back to my notes.

---

We don't speak again.

By the time I close my notebook an hour later he is still there, working with the specific focus of someone who has put something down and picked something else up instead. A useful skill. I notice it the way I notice most things — as data first, then as something that might be worth keeping.

I don't learn his name. He doesn't ask mine.

Outside, the canal is grey and the air has the particular cold of late November in Berlin — less damp than Amsterdam's, more decided, as if the cold here has made up its mind.

I open my notebook on the pavement.

*Coffee shop, Pannierstraße. TV: Mekong Delta flooding, third event this season. Man, around 30, dark jacket. Said: "Same pattern. Different coordinates." Following it without writing about it. Knows how to carry a thing.*

I read it back. Then I turn the page, because that is the discipline — note it, move on.

I walk home along the canal. The long way.

I'm not sure why I wrote it down.
