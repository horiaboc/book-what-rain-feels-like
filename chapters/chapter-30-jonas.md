# Chapter Thirty: The Second Corner

---

The man was on the corner of the Elandsgracht when we came out of the print shop in the morning. I didn't register him then — late thirties, grey jacket, unremarkable in the way of someone who has thought about being unremarkable. He was looking at his phone.

The print shop had been Iris's suggestion for the morning — a place she had mentioned visiting as a student, on the Elandsgracht, and which she thought she remembered well. It was there. It matched, mostly: the smell of it, the rows of type cases along the left wall, the owner with the opinions about paper weights who had talked to me for twenty minutes while Iris stood at the type cases and ran her fingers along the letters with an expression I had learned to read as *measuring*.

The owner's name was different from the one in her memory. She thought she'd known the previous owner, an older man. This man was his son; the father had retired two years ago. Possible, she said, on the way out. Possible I simply misremember the handover.

"Possible," I said.

We went south along the Elandsgracht toward the canal. I glanced back once, habit from nothing in particular, and the man in the grey jacket was still on the corner. Still looking at his phone.

I filed it and we kept walking.

---

The De Correspondent visit had already told us what we needed to know about her professional past in Amsterdam — thin documentation, vague warm recall from colleagues, specifics that slid slightly off-alignment. We spent the morning on the student neighbourhood: the Leidseplein, the streets behind the Paradiso where she had lived in her first year.

The street was right. The building she described — third floor, east-facing, a broken bicycle rack at the entrance — had a third floor and an east side, but the bicycle rack was on the other side of the door, and had apparently always been, according to a woman on the ground floor who had lived there eleven years.

Iris noted it. We moved on.

The canal route she had described to me in Berlin — the walk from her flat to the journalism school, the one she said she could do with her eyes closed — went wrong at the same turning it had gone wrong before, when we walked it in the first week. She stopped at the junction and said: "I keep taking this left."

"And it doesn't connect?"

"It connects to a street that dead-ends at a building site." She stood at the corner, looking both ways. "I don't understand why I take this left."

"What's to the right?"

"A bridge. The way I should go."

She stood there for a moment longer — not distressed, exactly. The quiet she has when she is computing something she doesn't yet want to give language to.

"My memory of this corner has a canal at the end of the left turning," she said. "A narrow one. With a wooden bridge."

We both looked left. Brick wall at the end, scaffolding.

"There's never been a canal there," I said.

"No."

---

We had lunch at a place near the Spui — a brown café, the kind that has been the same since 1970, dark wood and reasonable food and the particular Amsterdam pub smell that is its own category. Iris ordered *broodjes* and ate them with the efficient attention she gives to food when she's thinking about something else.

I was doing the map in my head. Entrance on the wrong side. Coffee machine with the wrong buttons. Thursday drinks that were Friday drinks. The letter on the wrong side of the building. The canal that wasn't there.

I said: "Can you tell me about the Leidseplein flat? Not the building — the inside."

She looked at me.

"The room," I said. "What you remember."

She described it. Single room, north-facing, which was cold, a skylight she had propped open in summer, a desk under the window, a bicycle in the corner because there was no storage. Specific and clear — the same quality of recall that had given me the canal detail.

"The skylight," I said. "What colour was the frame?"

"White," she said, immediately. "Chipped. I remember thinking I would repaint it."

"Did you?"

"No, I — no." She stopped. "I never did get around to that."

I watched her register that she couldn't hold the memory of not having repainted the skylight without an original experience of meaning to. You can remember intending something. You cannot remember the specific quality of the not-having-done-it without the time having passed.

She knew exactly what she'd noticed.

---

I saw the man again at three o'clock.

We were on the Singel, coming out of a bookshop — Iris had wanted to check whether the Athenaeum had a second location she remembered on this side of the canal, which it did not. Coming out the door, I looked left along the street and there he was. Grey jacket. Phone in hand.

Same posture. Same angle of the head.

This was not a coincidence. The Elandsgracht and the Singel are not the same neighbourhood. He had moved.

I said nothing to Iris. I took her arm as if I were steering us toward something interesting on the right and moved us that way. We walked east, quickly but not urgently.

At the next bridge I said: "Take the bridge, then left."

She looked at me. She read my face.

She took the bridge, then left.

I didn't look back. We walked for five minutes, then took a turn into a courtyard — the kind Amsterdam is full of, sudden green space behind a gate — and waited.

No one came through the gate.

I counted two minutes. Then we walked out the other end of the courtyard and flagged a tram.

---

On the tram, sitting at the back, Iris said: "The corner."

"And the Singel," I said. "Same man."

She was quiet.

"It might be nothing," I said. "It's probably not nothing."

"Connected to the hospital?"

"I don't know what else it would be connected to."

She looked out the tram window. "What do we do?"

"We keep going," I said. "But we pay attention." I thought for a moment. "And we don't go back to the same places twice."

She nodded.

"The Bloemgracht flat," I said. "If they know the hospital, they may know where we're staying."

"Do we move?"

I thought about it. The flat was paid through the week. Our things were there. More importantly, the Bloemgracht address was not in Iris's constructed memory — it had no connection to anything we were retracing. It was just a flat we had booked online.

"Not yet," I said. "But we pay attention."

She looked at me sideways. "You've done this before?"

"Found anomalies in systems and then been careful about what I touched?"

"Yes."

"More or less," I said.

She didn't quite smile. But there was something in her face that was adjacent to it — something that had to do, I thought, with the specific relief of not being the only person in the room who is taking a thing seriously.

We rode the tram back to the Jordaan, got off one stop early, and walked the last five minutes along a route neither of us had used before.

The flat was as we'd left it. The canal was where it had always been. I made coffee and Iris sat at the kitchen table and opened her notebook and started writing things down, which is what she does when she needs to make the shape of something visible.

I looked at her writing in her notebook and thought: someone built this person. With care. With enormous, particular care. And whoever it was is still somewhere, and the things they built are now sitting in a kitchen in Amsterdam making notes in a journal, and the notes are real, and the care is real, and the person is real.

Whatever that person is.
