# Chapter Thirty-One: De Reiger

---

Iris had mentioned the restaurant in passing, weeks before Amsterdam — a place in the Jordaan she had gone to with classmates, unremarkable from the outside, the kind that doesn't need to announce itself. De Reiger, on the Nieuwe Leliestraat. She had described the food the way she describes things she genuinely remembers: specifically, without performance. The stamppot. A particular table near the window.

We found it without difficulty. The outside was exactly as she had described — a narrow brown café front, the name in simple lettering above the door, light coming warm through the glass. She stopped at the entrance for a moment, reading the building the way she had been reading buildings all week.

"Well?" I said.

"It's right," she said. And then, after a beat: "The awning used to be green."

I looked. The awning was dark red.

"Could have changed," I said.

"Could have," she said. She pushed the door open. "Come on."

---

Inside was warm and unhurried — dark wood, close tables, the sound of a kitchen doing its work behind a half-open door. We were shown to a table near the window, which was not the specific table she remembered but close enough that she seemed satisfied. She ordered the stamppot. I ordered something with too much mustard that I did not regret.

The wine was good. The candle on the table was at the stage of its life where it has committed to a particular lean and is going to see it through.

We did not speak about Mara Seyn immediately. We let the food arrive, let the restaurant settle around us, let the evening be an evening for a few minutes before it became a discussion.

Then Iris said: "She knew about the OLVG before we told her anything."

"Yes."

"She knew my name. My Dutch identity number, presumably, if she accessed the health record."

"Yes."

"And she had someone following us for at least two days before she approached."

"At least," I said.

Iris looked at the window — the Nieuwe Leliestraat outside, a couple walking a dog, the ordinary Jordaan evening going about its business. "The offer is real," she said. "Or constructed to appear real. Either way, she wants something."

"The genetic panel," I said. "The tissue samples."

"Or she already knows what she'd find and wants confirmation." She picked up her fork. "Or she wants us somewhere private with her people."

We sat with that for a moment.

"The UMC appointment is tomorrow," I said.

"Yes."

"Real hospital. Real doctors. Whatever they find, it goes into a system with oversight, with records, with rights attached to it."

She looked at me. "You've already decided."

"I think you've already decided," I said. "I'm just saying it back clearly."

She was quiet for a moment. Then something in her face settled — not relief exactly, more like the particular stillness of a decision that has been made and can now stop being made.

"The UMC," she said.

"The UMC," I said.

We finished the stamppot. The candle continued its lean. Outside the window the Jordaan did what it has always done, which is to be itself without apology, which is a quality I have always found admirable in a neighbourhood.

---

On the way out I stopped at the bin on the corner of the Nieuwe Leliestraat and dropped Mara Seyn's card into it. Iris watched me do it. She said nothing, which was the right response, and we walked on.

---

Across the street and thirty metres back, a woman stood at the window of a closed flower shop, looking at her phone. She was not looking at her phone. She watched the man drop something into the bin. She noted the time. She sent a message of four words and put the phone away and walked in the opposite direction, unhurried, until the street had swallowed her completely.
