# Chapter Thirty-Four: Maastricht

---

Maastricht is the southernmost city in the Netherlands and feels like it — the architecture has a different weight, sandstone where the rest of the country uses brick, the river wide and serious, the streets older. There are Roman ruins under the city center and you can feel their mass through the soles of your shoes, the sense of something very old being held steady by accumulated weight.

Iris had mentioned Maastricht in passing once, in Berlin, not as a significant memory — just a weekend she'd spent here at some point during her student years, walking the city, visiting a bookshop she'd liked on the Grote Staat. The kind of place you mention without thinking about it because it's simply part of the geography of your life.

That was the point, I had realized. The memories she mentioned *without thinking about them* were the ones designed to live in the background — to create density, the felt sense of a life with years and texture. The ones she mentioned carefully, with detail, were the ones someone had built with care. The background ones were lighter. More likely to have errors.

We walked from the station into the city center. Iris navigated. She moved through the streets with the body-knowledge she'd described on the train: not looking for things, finding them. That skill, whatever it was, was consistent.

"The bookshop was here," she said, stopping on the Grote Staat.

We looked. A clothing shop.

"Could have closed," I said.

"Could have." She looked at the surrounding buildings. "Although — I remember it between those two." The façades on either side were what they were. The gap was right for a small bookshop. "What's the address?"

I looked it up. There was a bookshop on the Grote Staat in Maastricht — *Dominicanen*, famous, in a converted thirteenth-century church. We found it two streets over. When we went in, Iris stood at the entrance for a moment.

"This isn't it," she said.

"No?"

"This is extraordinary," she said, looking up at the vaulted ceiling, the medieval arches, the shelves built into what had once been a nave. "But it isn't the one I remember."

"What did yours look like?"

"Small. A bit dark. Owner with a large dog."

The Dominicanen is vast and lit and famous. It has been here for decades. It is, emphatically, not a small dark shop with a dog.

"Someone got the bookshop wrong," I said.

"Yes."

"But they got the street right."

She thought about it. "Maybe they looked up bookshops in Maastricht. Found the famous one and put me near it without knowing what it was like inside."

"Working from a distance," I said.

"Yes."

---

We ate dinner at a place near the Sint Servaasbasiliek — an old brasserie, the kind that has been feeding people since before anyone now living was born. Iris had game stew; I had something involving the Maastricht cheese they are apparently proud of. Both were good. We had wine. The evening had the specific quality that evenings have when you're tired and the day has been productive and the thing you're working on is still unsettled but you've moved it forward.

I had been working on the documentation trail in parallel all week — not aggressively, carefully, with the specific caution of someone who was increasingly aware that someone else was working on finding us. I had found the legal entity — a small company registered in Luxembourg, an unusual corporate structure with no public-facing activity. I had found the document assembly: Iris's Dutch identity had been generated through a chain of micro-task platforms, each step disconnected from the others. Someone had hired lawyers through one platform, translators through another, a document notary through a third. No single person had visibility into the whole.

It was elegant, I had thought, looking at it. And it was the kind of elegant that required not just resources but a specific understanding of how these systems worked — the gaps between them, the ways that distributed task platforms prevented any one person from understanding what they were building.

Someone who thought like an engineer, and moved like one.

I was sitting with this when the door of the brasserie opened.

---

I saw him before he saw us. A man in his early forties, the careful ordinariness of someone whose job is to be unremarkable — good at his job, almost. He came in as if looking for a table, scanned the room with the practiced peripheral sweep of someone who has learned to do it without looking like they're doing it. His eyes moved across us and stayed.

I said, very quietly: "Don't look at the door."

Iris was looking at her glass. "I know," she said. "He came in behind a couple."

She had clocked him before I said anything.

"How do you want to do this?" I said.

"The couple are still at the door," she said. "Waiting for a table. When the host takes them, we go to the bathroom — there's a corridor at the back left. I saw a fire exit."

I hadn't seen a fire exit. I had not, in fact, looked for a fire exit when we sat down.

"How do you know there's a fire exit?"

"There's always a fire exit in a building this age," she said. "And I can smell cold air from the left side of the corridor."

I thought about this for a second and decided to accept it.

The host took the couple to a table. We stood up — I left money, the right amount, already — and walked toward the bathroom sign. The man at the door moved. Iris moved faster.

The corridor was there. The fire exit was there, a grey door at the end, the push-bar kind. Iris hit it without breaking stride and we came out into an alley that smelled of kitchen waste and cold stone.

She went right without hesitating. I followed. Behind us, the fire door opened again.

Two of them now.

Iris turned left through a gap between buildings — this was not a passage, exactly, more of a maintenance gap, barely wide enough, the stone wall of the basilica on the right — and I followed, and behind me I heard the first man try to follow and not make it through without turning sideways, which cost him time.

We came out onto an open square, the basilica lit above us, tourists with cameras, a group of students. Iris walked straight into the students — excuse me, pardon — and through them, and I stayed with her, and by the time we came out the other side of the group we were different shapes in a different cluster of people.

She walked me through two more streets at a pace that looked like two people with somewhere to go, and then we were on the river embankment and the city was behind us and the Maas was in front of us and there was nobody.

---

We stood at the railing above the river for a moment.

The Maas is wide here, the far bank France, the city reflected in the water in the way of rivers that have been looked at long enough to learn what to do with the light.

I was out of breath. Iris was not.

I looked at her. She was looking at the river, her hands on the railing, her breathing completely normal.

I said: "The gap in the wall."

"Yes."

"You timed the width exactly."

"The first man was roughly my shoulder width plus fifteen centimetres," she said. "The gap was narrower than that with his coat on."

She said this as if it were a perfectly ordinary thing to have calculated in half a second in a dark alley while moving.

I said: "The krav maga."

She glanced at me. "Among other things."

I leaned on the railing. The river went past below. France over there, its lights beginning to show in the dusk.

I said: "Whatever you are."

She looked at me.

"I just want to be clear," I said. "Whatever you are, the part of you that just walked me through a maintenance gap using shoulder-width calculations — I find it genuinely impressive and I would like you to know that."

She looked at the river. There was something happening in her expression — relief and amusement and something more private, held just inside the face.

"Also," I said, "if it turns out you are from some other context entirely — some other — I'm just saying. I'd be fine with it. We'd figure it out."

"An alien," she said.

"I'm still leaving it open."

"Jonas."

"Empirically," I said, "I cannot rule it out."

She made the sound — the one that had come out of her in the park, surprised out of her, more real for being unexpected. A proper laugh. It came off the river and the stone walls and went somewhere.

Then she put her forehead against my shoulder, briefly, and I put my arm around her and we stood there above the Maas with the lights coming on and France going dark across the water.

"Heerlen tomorrow," she said, after a moment.

"Heerlen tomorrow," I said.

She straightened. She looked at the river. "I know what we'll find."

"Maybe."

"I know," she said. Quiet. Certain. Not broken — just the thing you sound like when you have accepted a truth before you've seen it confirmed.

I kept my arm where it was.

Below us the Maas went on, wide and dark and old, carrying everything south the way rivers do, indifferent and faithful, the same river it had been before any of this and would be after.
